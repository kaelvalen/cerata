\begin{abstract}
Users of deployed language models often provide facts that the model should retain.
Knowledge editing methods write such facts into the model weights, but they do not
support removing an individual fact, identifying which stored fact produced an answer,
or limiting the effect of an update on unrelated inputs. We present Cerata, which keeps
the base model frozen and stores each fact as a separate low-rank adapter. An adapter is
trained with a KL penalty toward the base model and is accepted only if its divergence
on a set of canary prompts stays below a threshold. A router without trainable
parameters applies at most one adapter to each query and uses the unmodified base model
otherwise. With this design, exact removal and answer attribution follow from the data
structure. We measure the remaining behavioral properties in a pre-registered study with
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and 1000 facts each from CounterFact and zsRE. With routing,
Cerata answers \cfEffPct\% and \zsEffPct\% of the edit prompts correctly, and the outputs
for \cfNoLeakPct\% and \zsNoLeakPct\% of distractor prompts contain no stored answer.
After 100 CounterFact facts are removed, \cfReturnPct\% of their prompts return to the
base model's answer, and \cfRetainPct\% of the remaining facts are still answered
correctly. A retrieval baseline instructed to follow its context answers 83.7\% of the
CounterFact prompts. Paraphrase accuracy on CounterFact is limited by the router's
similarity threshold, which we analyze in detail.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Knowledge editing, machine unlearning, low-rank adaptation, mixture of experts,

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{U}{sers} of deployed language models often provide information that the
model should keep: corrections of outdated answers, facts about entities that were
absent from the training data, and user-specific details that should persist across
sessions. We use the term \emph{living model} for a model that accumulates such
knowledge during deployment. If the knowledge is written into the model parameters,
three practical requirements follow. An operator must be able to remove a specific
fact on request, which data protection regulation can make mandatory~\cite{gdpr2016}.
It should be possible to determine which stored fact produced a given answer. Finally,
an update should not change the model's behavior on unrelated inputs.

Knowledge editing methods write facts into the weights by solving for an update of
selected layers~\cite{meng2022rome,meng2023memit,fang2025alphaedit}, by training an
editor network~\cite{decao2021editing,mitchell2022mend}, or by adding adapters and side
memories for sequential edits~\cite{huang2023tpatcher,hartvigsen2023grace,wang2024wise}.
These methods are evaluated on edit success, paraphrase generalization and locality.
Sequential editing degrades earlier edits~\cite{gupta2024forgetting} and general
abilities~\cite{gu2024harms}, and teacher-forced evaluation overestimates edit
success~\cite{yang2025mirage}. Removing a single edit from weights that hold many edits
is an unlearning problem, for which current methods are approximate and difficult to
verify~\cite{eldan2023harrypotter,maini2024tofu,shi2025muse,liu2025rethinking}.
Retrieval-augmented generation~\cite{lewis2020rag} avoids these issues by keeping facts
outside the model, so removal is an index deletion. Its limitation is that the model
must follow the retrieved text, and instruction-tuned models often do not when the text
contradicts their parametric knowledge~\cite{longpre2021conflicts,xie2024chameleon}.

We treat additions to and removals from the model's stored knowledge as transactions in
the database sense~\cite{haerder1983transactions}: an addition is accepted only if it
passes a check, a removal returns the store to its earlier state, and each operation is
logged. Three design choices make these properties hold. The base model is never
updated, each fact is stored as its own low-rank adapter, and a router without
trainable parameters applies at most one adapter to a query. With these choices, exact
removal and answer attribution are properties of the data structure, which we state as
propositions in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. The behavioral properties, namely whether
stored facts are answered, whether unrelated prompts are unaffected, and whether
removal restores the base model's answers, must be measured.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item We formalize the store and its operations and identify which guarantees follow
from the design and which require measurement (Section~\ref{sec:problem}).
\item We report the exploratory measurements that determined the design: independently
trained adapters fail when summed, a KL penalty reduces their effect on unrelated
prompts by an order of magnitude, and routing requires both a sentence encoder and an
entity filter (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).
\item We evaluate the system with 1000 facts each from CounterFact and zsRE and compare
it with retrieval and the official GRACE and WISE implementations
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item We report small-scale experiments on deciding which facts to store, on updates
across sessions, and on a vision-language model (Section~\ref{sec:loop}).
\end{enumerate}
All experiments were pre-registered. While preparing this paper we found that our
earlier evaluation script served each edit prompt with its own adapter whenever the
router selected any adapter. We re-evaluated the stored adapters with the router's
actual selection. Efficacy changed by at most 0.2 percentage points, and all numbers in
this paper use the corrected evaluation.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Knowledge Editing}
ROME and MEMIT locate MLP layers associated with factual recall and compute a weight
update for them~\cite{meng2022rome,meng2023memit}. AlphaEdit restricts the update to the
null space of preserved knowledge~\cite{fang2025alphaedit}. KE and MEND train a network
that maps a fine-tuning gradient to an edit~\cite{decao2021editing,mitchell2022mend}.
Several methods for sequential editing add parameters instead of changing existing
weights. T-Patcher adds neurons for each mistake~\cite{huang2023tpatcher}, GRACE stores
discrete key-value pairs with a deferral radius~\cite{hartvigsen2023grace}, WISE routes
between the original weights and a side memory that is periodically
merged~\cite{wang2024wise}, and MELO, LEMoE and ELDER attach LoRA blocks selected by an
index or a router~\cite{yu2024melo,wang2024lemoe,li2025elder}. Benchmarks and
toolkits~\cite{yao2023editing,wang2024easyedit} measure efficacy, generalization and
locality. Later work studies ripple effects and multi-hop
consistency~\cite{cohen2024ripple,zhong2023mquake}, forgetting over long edit
sequences~\cite{gupta2024forgetting}, damage to general abilities~\cite{gu2024harms},
and the difference between teacher-forced and free generation~\cite{yang2025mirage}.
Cerata uses the same architecture as MELO, with per-fact LoRA blocks behind an index. It
differs in the operations it provides around that architecture: a divergence check at
write time, removal of individual facts, and attribution of answers to stored facts.
Table~\ref{tab:positioning} compares these operations across approaches.

\input{tables/positioning}

\subsection{Retrieval and In-Context Knowledge}
Retrieval-augmented generation~\cite{lewis2020rag}, nearest-neighbor language
models~\cite{khandelwal2020knnlm} and in-context editing~\cite{zheng2023ike} keep new
knowledge outside the weights, as do SERAC, which combines a scope classifier with a
counterfactual model~\cite{mitchell2022serac}, and RECIPE, which retrieves continuous
prompts~\cite{chen2024recipe}. MemGPT manages such memory across
sessions~\cite{packer2023memgpt}. Because models tend to prefer parametric knowledge
over conflicting context~\cite{longpre2021conflicts,xie2024chameleon}, our retrieval
baseline includes an explicit instruction to follow the context.

\subsection{Adapters, Merging and Routing}
LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} and its variants~\cite{hayou2024loraplus,liu2024dora,
meng2024pissa,buyukakyuz2024olora} keep per-fact parameter sets small, and serving
systems such as S-LoRA hold thousands of adapters~\cite{sheng2024slora}. Adapters can be
combined by addition~\cite{ilharco2023taskarithmetic}, merged with interference
resolution~\cite{yadav2023ties}, composed per input~\cite{huang2024lorahub,
zhao2024loraretriever} or mixed by a gate~\cite{wu2024mole,shazeer2017moe}. OPPU trains
one adapter per user for personalization~\cite{tan2024oppu}, and Schneider et al.\ keep
per-user components separate from shared weights so that they can be
deleted~\cite{schneider2026separable}. The failure of summed adapters that we observe
(Section~\ref{sec:lessons}) is an instance of the interference studied in the merging
literature.

\subsection{Machine Unlearning}
Exact unlearning removes the influence of deleted data completely. Cao and Yang express
the learner as summations that can be updated~\cite{cao2015forget}, Ginart et al.\ give
deletion-efficient algorithms for $k$-means~\cite{ginart2019forget}, and SISA retrains
only the shard that contained the deleted data~\cite{bourtoule2021sisa}. Unlearning for
large language models is approximate in general~\cite{eldan2023harrypotter} and is
evaluated with forget and retain sets~\cite{maini2024tofu,shi2025muse,
liu2025rethinking}. In mixture-of-experts models, an update to the router can redirect
queries away from the expert that holds the knowledge without removing it, and SEUF and
GRIP constrain the router to prevent this~\cite{zhuang2025seuf,zhu2026grip}. Cerata
resembles SISA with one shard per fact. Its router has no trainable state, so removing a
fact cannot change routing decisions for queries unrelated to it
(Proposition~\ref{prop:locality}).

\subsection{Memory-Augmented and Self-Updating Models}
Larimar adds an episodic memory with one-shot writes and selective
forgetting~\cite{das2024larimar}, Titans learns a neural memory at test
time~\cite{behrouz2025titans}, and SEAL generates its own training data to update its
weights~\cite{zweiger2025seal}. These systems change during use but do not provide
removal of individual items or attribution. Our KL penalty corresponds to the
distillation term of Learning without Forgetting~\cite{li2018lwf}, and storing one
adapter per fact is a form of parameter isolation~\cite{rusu2016progressive}. For
monolithic models, attributing outputs to training data requires influence
approximations~\cite{koh2017influence,grosse2023influence}. With one adapter per fact,
attribution reduces to reading the router's decision.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Store and Operations}

Let $f_{\theta_0}$ be a pre-trained language model with parameters $\theta_0$ that are
never updated. A fact $e$ has a subject string $s_e$, an edit prompt $x_e$, a target
answer $a_e$ and a set of training pairs $P(e)$. The stored knowledge is
\begin{equation}
S=\{(i,\ \delta_i,\ k_i,\ s_i)\}_{i\in\mathcal{I}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{I}$ is the set of active identifiers, $\delta_i$ is a low-rank parameter
update, and $k_i$ is a routing key computed by a frozen sentence encoder $E$. A router
$\rho_S$ maps a query $q$ to an identifier in $\mathcal{I}$ or to $\bot$. The system
answers
\begin{equation}
\serve_S(q)=\big(f_{\theta_0\oplus\delta_{\rho_S(q)}}(q),\ \rho_S(q)\big),
\end{equation}
where $\theta_0\oplus\delta_\bot=\theta_0$. At most one update is applied to any query,
and the second component reports which one. \commit$(e)$ trains a candidate update,
measures its divergence from the base model and either inserts it or rejects it.
\revoke$(i)$ deletes entry $i$. Each operation is recorded in a log together with the
resulting state hash
\begin{equation}
h(S)=H\big(h_0\ \|\ (i,\delta_i)_{i\in\mathcal{I},\ \text{sorted by } i}\big),
\end{equation}
where $H$ is SHA-256 and $h_0$ is a digest of the base configuration.

\subsection{Requirements}

We evaluate the store against five requirements.
\begin{itemize}
\item[R1] After \commit$(e)$, the answer to $x_e$ contains $a_e$, and preferably so does
the answer to a paraphrase of $x_e$.
\item[R2] Queries outside the scope of the store receive the base model's answer.
\item[R3] An addition is accepted only if its divergence from the base model is below a
threshold, and a rejected or failed addition leaves the store unchanged.
\item[R4] After \revoke$(i)$, the store equals the store before \commit$(e_i)$, and the
answer to $x_{e_i}$ returns to the base model's answer.
\item[R5] Each answer identifies the stored fact that produced it, and that fact is
necessary for the answer.
\end{itemize}
R1, R2, the behavioral part of R4 and the necessity part of R5 must be measured. The
remaining parts follow from the design, as shown next.

\subsection{Properties of the Design}
\label{sec:props}

The router (Section~\ref{sec:router}) computes a candidate set
$C_S(q)\subseteq\mathcal{I}$ from string matches between $q$ and the stored subjects. It
returns $j^\star=\arg\max_{j\in C_S(q)}\cos(E(q),k_j)$ if this similarity is at least
$\tau$, and $\bot$ otherwise. Keys are computed once from each fact and are not trained.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:identity}
The state hash and the function $\serve_S$ depend only on the set of active entries. In
particular, for a fact added as $i\notin\mathcal{I}$,
$h(\revoke_i(\commit_i(S)))=h(S)$ and $\serve_{\revoke_i(\commit_i(S))}=\serve_S$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
$h$ hashes the active entries in identifier order, and $\serve_S$ reads only active
entries. \revoke$_i$ deletes the entry that \commit$_i$ inserted.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:locality}
Let $S'=S\cup\{(i,\delta_i,k_i,s_i)\}$. For every query $q$ with $i\notin C_{S'}(q)$,
$\serve_{S'}(q)=\serve_S(q)$. Adding or removing fact $i$ therefore changes the output
only for queries in which $i$ is a candidate.
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
If a subject in $S'$ occurs exactly in $q$, $C_{S'}(q)$ contains the longest such
subject. If that subject is not $s_i$, it is also the longest exact match in $S$, so
$C_S(q)=C_{S'}(q)$. Otherwise $C_{S'}(q)$ is the set of approximate token matches, and
$i\notin C_{S'}(q)$ again implies $C_S(q)=C_{S'}(q)$. With equal candidate sets and fixed
keys, the router returns the same result, and the selected update is unchanged.
\end{IEEEproof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:atomic}
\commit\ computes the key, trains the candidate on a separate copy of $\theta_0$ and
measures its divergence before it modifies the store. A rejection, or an exception
raised before the check, leaves $S$, the log and $h(S)$ unchanged.
\end{proposition}

These statements have three limitations. Equality of outputs assumes deterministic
inference; on GPU we observed identical greedy outputs but did not verify identical
logits. Proposition~\ref{prop:atomic} does not cover failures during insertion after
the check. Finally, removal applies to the serving state only. It does not erase copies
in backups and does not affect what the frozen base model already knows, which is why
R4 also requires the answer to return to the base model's answer.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\input{figures/architecture}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} gives an overview of the system, and Algorithm~\ref{alg:txn} lists
its operations.

\subsection{Per-Fact Adapters}

Each fact is stored as a rank-16 LoRA update~\cite{hu2022lora} of the query and value
projections in every layer. It is trained from a fresh copy of the base model on two
pairs, a statement of the fact and the edit prompt with its answer
(Table~\ref{tab:hparams}). The loss is
\begin{multline}
\mathcal{L}(\delta)=\sum_{(u,v)\in P(e)}\mathrm{CE}\big(v\mid u;\theta_0\oplus\delta\big)\\
+\frac{\lambda}{|Q_c|}\sum_{c\in Q_c}\mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\theta_0}(\cdot\mid c)\,\big\|\,
p_{\theta_0\oplus\delta}(\cdot\mid c)\big),
\label{eq:loss}
\end{multline}
where $Q_c$ is a set of canary prompts and the KL term is computed on the next-token
distribution. Without the KL term, training settings that learn the facts reliably also
change the canary distributions substantially (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).

\subsection{Divergence Check}

Let $p_c=p_{\theta_0}(\cdot\mid c)$ and $\tilde p_c=p_{\theta_0\oplus\delta}(\cdot\mid c)$. A
candidate is scored by the mean symmetric KL divergence on the canary prompts,
\begin{equation}
D(\delta)=\frac{1}{|Q_c|}\sum_{c\in Q_c}\big[\mathrm{KL}(p_c\,\|\,\tilde p_c)
+\mathrm{KL}(\tilde p_c\,\|\,p_c)\big],
\end{equation}
and it is inserted only if $D(\delta)\le\kappa$. In our experiments the same canary prompts
are used for the KL term and for the check, so $D$ measures the change on those prompts
and is not an estimate of general capability. Queries unrelated to stored facts are
protected by the router, which passes them to the unmodified base model. The check
limits the damage when the router selects an adapter incorrectly.

\subsection{Router}
\label{sec:router}

The router has no trainable parameters. It first normalizes the query and the stored
subjects (lower case, with hyphens and underscores replaced by spaces) and searches for
subjects that occur in the query, keeping the longest. If none occurs, it accepts
subjects that share a token of at least four characters with the query within an
optimal string alignment distance of 1, or 2 for tokens of six or more characters. If
no subject matches, the router returns $\bot$. Otherwise a frozen multilingual sentence
encoder~\cite{reimers2019sbert,reimers2020multilingual} compares the query with the keys
of the candidates, where each key is the embedding of the fact's edit prompt. The best
candidate is selected if its cosine similarity is at least $\tau$. The entity filter is
needed because the sentence embeddings of the same question about two different
subjects are very similar (Section~\ref{sec:lessons}).

\subsection{Removal, Update and Logging}

Each adapter is stored as its pair of LoRA factors together with its key and subject.
For serving, the selected adapter is written into a LoRA slot of the model and all other
slots are set to zero. \revoke$(i)$ deletes the entry. An update is implemented as a
removal followed by an addition. The log records the identifier, the outcome of the
check, $D$, the training time and the state hash. Attribution is checked by leaving one
adapter out: an answer is attributed to fact $i$ if the target appears when $\delta_i$ is
active and does not appear when it is suspended.

The system is implemented in Python on Hugging Face Transformers and PEFT, and adapters
are stored in fp32. In the version evaluated here, the log consists of the records kept
by the experiment scripts, and the leave-one-out check is a separate script. We have not
built a persistent audit service.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Operations on the store}
\label{alg:txn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Function{Add}{$e$, $i$}
  \State $k\gets E(x_e)$
  \State $\delta\gets\arg\min\mathcal{L}$ on a fresh copy of $\theta_0$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}}
  \State $d\gets D(\delta)$
  \If{$d>\kappa$} \State log$(\textsc{reject},i,d)$ and \Return \EndIf
  \State $S\gets S\cup\{(i,\delta,k,s_e)\}$; log$(\textsc{add},i,d,h(S))$
\EndFunction
\Function{Serve}{$q$}
  \State $j\gets\rho_S(q)$
  \State \Return $\big(f_{\theta_0\oplus\delta_j}(q),\ j\big)$ \Comment{$\delta_\bot=0$}
\EndFunction
\Function{Remove}{$i$}
  \State $S\gets S\setminus\{i\}$; log$(\textsc{remove},i,h(S))$
\EndFunction
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Exploratory Study}
\label{sec:lessons}

The design was developed in a sequence of small experiments, each registered before it
was run. Table~\ref{tab:lessons} summarizes the measurements that determined each design
choice, and this section describes four of them.

\input{tables/design_lessons}

\subsection{Summing Adapters}
The simplest store would keep the sum of all adapters in a single LoRA module, so that
removal would be a subtraction. This did not work. With three facts on the 1.5B model,
adding the second adapter erased the first fact, and adding the third erased all three.
With 18 facts the sum answered none of them, and with 50 CounterFact or zsRE facts it
answered 0\% and 2\%. The same adapters, applied one at a time by a router, answered all
18. A single adapter trained sequentially on all facts answered 8\% of synthetic facts
and 34\% of CounterFact facts (80\% on zsRE, where the base model already knows many
answers) and changed the answers to distractors. The problem is not the capacity of a
rank-16 update, since one adapter trained jointly on 256 CounterFact facts answers all
of them (Section~\ref{sec:capacity}). We attribute the failure to interference between
independently trained updates~\cite{ilharco2023taskarithmetic,yadav2023ties} and
therefore apply one adapter per query.

\subsection{Training Strength and the KL Penalty}
With 18 routed adapters, a weak setting (learning rate $10^{-4}$, 8 steps) changed the
canary distributions little (mean symmetric KL 0.18) but did not learn the facts
(leave-one-out attribution 6\%). The setting that learned every fact ($3\cdot10^{-4}$,
16 steps) produced a mean divergence of 10.96 and a maximum of 22.1. Adding the KL term
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} with $\lambda=1$ kept the facts and reduced the mean divergence to
0.93. The one attribution failure in that run was a fact whose answer, ``30'', the base
model also produced. With a distinctive answer, attribution was 100\%.

\subsection{Routing Keys}
On held-out paraphrases of six facts, TF-IDF character n-grams routed one correctly, and
the similarity of a paraphrase to its own key was lower than to the best other key. The
last-token hidden state of the base model routed three, because all short queries had
similarities above 0.9 to all keys. A multilingual MiniLM sentence encoder separated the
keys (minimum own-key similarity 0.742, maximum other-key similarity 0.569) and routed
five of six, and all six when one paraphrase pair was added to training.

\subsection{Entity Filter}
In the first 50-fact pilot, every distractor that asked the same question about an
unseen subject was routed to an adapter, since the question template dominates the
sentence embedding. Requiring a known subject before comparing embeddings raised
abstention from 0\% to 100\% without reducing routing accuracy on edit prompts or
paraphrases. The approximate match described in Section~\ref{sec:router} was added later
to handle spelling variants (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix}, Table~\ref{tab:router}).

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Model and Data}
The base model is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2024qwen25}, used in bf16 with its
chat template on a single 8\,GB GPU. Facts are drawn from the ROME versions of
CounterFact~\cite{meng2022rome} and zsRE~\cite{levy2017zeroshot,decao2021editing}.
CounterFact provides counterfactual targets that the base model does not produce. For
zsRE we use the reference answer, which the base model often knows already, so zsRE
tests storing and removing facts that overlap with the model's knowledge. We keep items
whose target is a single token and whose paraphrase contains the subject, shuffle the
remaining items with seed $N$ and take the first $N$. Each fact has an edit prompt, a
held-out paraphrase (the first paraphrase prompt in CounterFact and the rephrased
question in zsRE) and a distractor. The distractor is the edit prompt with the subject
replaced by one of five synthetic names that do not occur in the store. A set of
synthetic facts with five relations is used for pilots and router tests.

\subsection{Metrics}
An answer is counted as correct if the lower-cased target occurs in the lower-cased
greedy output. Efficacy and paraphrase accuracy are computed with routing. Route
accuracy is the fraction of edit prompts routed to their own fact, and abstention is the
fraction of distractors for which the router returns $\bot$. A distractor shows leakage
if its output contains a stored answer that the base model's output for the same
distractor does not contain. For removal, we remove the first 100 facts and measure
three quantities: the fraction of their edit prompts that no longer contain the target
(gone), the fraction whose correctness equals that of the base model before training
(return), and the accuracy on the 900 remaining facts (retention). The divergence $D$ is
recorded for every addition. Confidence intervals are 95\% percentile bootstrap
intervals over facts with 2000 resamples~\cite{efron1994bootstrap}.

\subsection{Baselines}
The retrieval baseline uses the same router to select a fact and inserts its statement
into the system prompt, either alone or with the instruction ``Use the context even if
it contradicts what you know.'' Question-answer formatting and a few-shot example
performed worse at $N=200$ (71\% and 52\%) and were not run at $N=1000$. GRACE and WISE
are the EasyEdit implementations~\cite{wang2024easyedit} with their Qwen2.5 settings
adapted to 28 layers (GRACE at layer 18 with 50 iterations, WISE at layer 23 with 70
iterations in retrieval mode). They were run in fp16 as 200 sequential edits on
CounterFact. WISE was run with its default schedule, which does not merge within 200
edits, and with a merge after edit 200. Both are scored with our protocol under the chat
template and on the plain edit prompt for which they were designed.

\subsection{Pre-Registration}
Arms, metrics, thresholds and predictions were committed before the first confirmatory
run, and later experiments, bug fixes and deviations were added to the record before
they were run. The thresholds at $N=1000$ were a route accuracy and an abstention of at
least 0.95, a retention of at least 0.98, a mean divergence of at most 0.05 and a
leave-one-out attribution of at least 0.95.

\subsection{Serving}
\label{sec:serving}

Table~\ref{tab:guarantees} lists the results at $N=1000$. With routing, the system

own adapter. On CounterFact, 997 of 1000 prompts are routed to their own fact, and the
three errors involve subjects that occur twice in the sample. On zsRE, route accuracy is
\zsRoutePct\%, which underestimates routing quality. The sample contains questions that
are rephrasings of one another: 360 facts share both subject and answer with another
fact. All 210 routing errors select such a fact, and 208 of them are answered correctly.
No subject in the sample has two facts with different answers, so the ability of the
router to separate conflicting facts about one subject is not tested.

Paraphrase accuracy is \zsParaPct\% on zsRE and \cfParaPct\% on CounterFact. We analyze
the CounterFact result in Section~\ref{sec:limits}. The router declines \cfAbstPct\% and
\zsAbstPct\% of distractors, and the outputs for \cfNoLeakPct\% and \zsNoLeakPct\% of
distractors contain no stored answer. All leaks occur when the approximate match links a
synthetic name to a real subject, for example ``Muku-75'' to ``Mukul Roy'' and
``Fapa-91'' to ``Scott Fava.'' Abstention meets the registered threshold on both sets.
Route accuracy meets it on CounterFact but not on zsRE, where the registered definition
counts routing to an equivalent fact as an error. We report this as a missed threshold.

Our earlier evaluation script served an edit prompt with its own adapter whenever the
router returned any candidate, so a routing error was counted as correct if the correct
adapter would have answered. Paraphrases, distractors and removal were already routed.
We re-evaluated the saved stores without retraining, using the router's selection for
every query and logging each route. The inputs were verified against SHA-256 hashes of
the stored adapters and fact files. Table~\ref{tab:guarantees} reports both versions.

\subsection{Removal}
\label{sec:revoke}

Removing the first 100 facts deletes their adapters. By
Proposition~\ref{prop:identity}, the resulting store is identical to one to which these
facts were never added. On CounterFact, the base model produced none of the 100 targets
before training. After removal, the target is absent for \cfGonePct\% of the prompts,
so the answer returns to the base model's answer in the same fraction of cases. In the
four remaining cases, the approximate match routes the prompt to another stored fact
with a similar subject and the same target, for example from ``Chevrolet Caprice'' to
``Chevrolet Chevette,'' both with the answer ``IBM.''

On zsRE the base model already answers 44 of the 100 prompts, so the absence of the
target (\zsGonePct\%) does not measure removal. The answer returns to the base model's
answer in \zsReturnPct\% of cases. In each of the other 20 cases the base model did not
give the target but the system still does after removal, and in 15 of them the prompt
is routed to a rephrased copy of the removed fact that is still stored. Removal applies
to one entry. Removing a fact that is stored under several phrasings requires removing
each copy, for example by removing all entries with the same subject and answer.

Retention on the 900 remaining facts, with routing, is \cfRetainPct\% on CounterFact and
\zsRetainPct\% on zsRE, above the threshold of 98\%. A removal takes about 2.5\,ms,
including resetting the serving slots.

\subsection{Divergence from the Base Model}
\label{sec:footprint}

Over all 1000 additions, the mean divergence $D$ is 0.022 on CounterFact and 0.018 on
zsRE, with 95th percentiles of 0.052 and 0.043 and maxima of 0.092 and 0.087. No
addition was rejected at $\kappa=2.0$. Recomputing $D$ for the first 50 adapters of each
saved store gives means of 0.0215 and 0.0171, which satisfies the registered threshold
of 0.05, although 5.5\% and 2.2\% of individual adapters exceed it. In the exploratory
phase the check did reject candidates. An adapter trained without the KL term had
$D=21.6$, and on the vision-language model an adapter trained to change the answer to a
canary question had $D=23.9$. Both were rejected and the state hash was unchanged.

\subsection{Attribution}
\label{sec:audit}

For the first 100 CounterFact facts, we served each edit prompt with its adapter,
suspended the adapter, served the prompt again and restored the adapter. The target was
present with the adapter and absent without it in all 100 cases (0.43\,s per fact), and
the state hash after the procedure equaled the hash before it.

\subsection{Comparison with Retrieval}

\input{figures/scaling}

With the same router, retrieval with a plain context answers 83.4\% of CounterFact edit
prompts at $N=1000$, and retrieval with the instruction to follow the context answers
83.7\% (95\% CI 81.5 to 86.0), compared with \cfEffPct\% for Cerata
(Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). We registered this comparison as a stopping condition: had
instructed retrieval matched the stored adapters, we would have withdrawn the efficacy
claim. On zsRE at $N=200$, retrieval answered 79\% and Cerata 99\% (each prompt served by
its own adapter). On synthetic facts, for which the base model has no prior answer, the
two performed similarly (80\% and 82\% at $N=50$). The advantage of stored adapters is
therefore specific to facts that conflict with the base model's knowledge. Paraphrase
accuracy on CounterFact is low for both systems (31\% and 32\%).

\subsection{Comparison with Editing Methods}

\input{tables/baselines}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares the official editing implementations with Cerata.
GRACE answers all 200 edits on the plain edit prompt but only 2.5\% under the chat
template, because its keys are activations of the last token of the plain prompt and
the chat template changes this token. WISE reports a per-edit accuracy of 100\%, while
the model after all 200 edits answers 7\% of them. With a merge after edit 200, the final
accuracy rises to 12.5\%, the fraction of distractors without leakage falls from 64\% to
27\%, and the accuracy on plain prompts is 5\%, so the decline is not caused by the chat
template. These results agree with earlier findings that editing results depend on the
evaluation format and on whether accuracy is measured after each edit or after all
edits~\cite{yang2025mirage,gupta2024forgetting}. Neither implementation provides an
operation for removing a single edit. Because GRACE and WISE were run with 200 facts and
Cerata with 1000, the comparison is descriptive.

\subsection{Grouped Adapters}
\label{sec:capacity}

\input{tables/capacity}

Each per-fact adapter occupies 8.7\,MB in fp32 (8.7\,GB at $N=1000$) and takes 8.5\,s
to train, whereas locate-then-edit methods add no parameters. One adapter of the same
rank trained jointly on $k$ CounterFact facts answers 98.4\%, 100\% and 100\% of its
facts at $k=64$, 128 and 256, with a divergence of at most 0.007
(Table~\ref{tab:capacity}). Its paraphrase accuracy is 58\%, 70\% and 66\%. A grouped
adapter also produces the answer for one to three of ten held-out facts that the base
model does not answer, and removing one of its facts requires retraining the group.
Grouping therefore reduces storage by up to a factor of 256 at the cost of coarser
removal and some leakage.

\subsection{Router and Adapter Variants}
\label{sec:routerres}

Table~\ref{tab:router} in the appendix reports routing accuracy under perturbations of
the subject on 50 synthetic facts. Exact substring matching fails for all perturbations
except lower case. The approximate match routes all misspellings and spacing variants
and 80\% of the prompts that contain only the first token of the subject, without
changing the results on unperturbed prompts and distractors. Routing with sentence
embeddings alone handles these perturbations but does not decline any distractor.
Prompts in which the subject is replaced by a pronoun are not routed, as expected for a
router without dialogue context.

Table~\ref{tab:recipes} in the appendix compares adapter training variants on 50
CounterFact facts, each served by its own adapter. LoRA+~\cite{hayou2024loraplus} gives
the highest paraphrase accuracy (90\% compared with 86\%) and the lowest divergence. DoRA
and OLoRA exceed the divergence threshold by an order of magnitude and would be
rejected. Adding the MLP projections increases the size without improving paraphrase
accuracy. PiSSA was not tested because it modifies the base weights. The stores
evaluated above were trained with the baseline variant before this comparison. LoRA+
was unstable when training grouped adapters with an eight times larger learning rate
for the $B$ factor, so we recommend it only for per-fact adapters.

\section{Extensions}
\label{sec:loop}

This section reports smaller experiments on three questions that arise when the store
is used during deployment: which facts should be stored as adapters, how updates behave
across sessions, and whether the approach applies to a vision-language model.
Table~\ref{tab:loop} summarizes the results. The experiments are exploratory and
involve few facts.

\input{tables/living_loop}

\subsection{Choosing Which Facts to Store}
We combined the adapter store with a retrieval memory in which notes can be written at
no cost, and used a controller to decide when a fact should receive an adapter. The
stream contains 6 facts that are queried three times and 12 that are queried once. A
rule that stores every fact queried at least twice created 6 adapters and answered 16 of
18 facts. The two failures were facts queried only once on which retrieval fails. Bandit
controllers that decide before the query is answered performed poorly. The
$\epsilon$-greedy controller never stored a fact, and UCB1~\cite{auer2002ucb} stored 17.
In this setting the controller never observes whether retrieval would have answered
correctly. A controller that first answers with retrieval and decides only after an
incorrect answer stored the three facts that retrieval answered incorrectly and
answered all 18.

\subsection{Updates Across Sessions}
On a stream with corrections and removal requests over two sessions, the combined
system applied updates (a removal followed by the addition of the corrected fact) and
removals, kept its two adapters across the session boundary, and answered all 17
remaining facts in the second session. In a separate run that applies an update to a
stored adapter, removing the updated adapter restored the hash of the empty store.

\subsection{Vision-Language Model}
We applied the same store to Qwen3-VL-2B~\cite{qwen2025qwen3vl}, with keys computed
from CLIP image embeddings~\cite{radford2021clip}, which separated our synthetic scenes
better than DINOv2~\cite{oquab2024dinov2}. Two facts keyed by images were answered,
distractor images were not routed, an adapter trained to change a canary answer was
rejected at $D=23.9$, updates and removals behaved as on text, and a saved store
reloaded with the same hash. An adapter trained only on the question and answer produced
the answer for any image. Adding training pairs that map the same question on other
images to ``I don't know'' made the adapter respond only to its own scene and to three
perturbed versions of it. With such adapters, the controller described above created
four adapters and answered all four facts without leakage.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\subsection{Paraphrases and Routing}
When served directly by their own adapters, 86\% to 90\% of CounterFact paraphrases are
answered correctly, and so are 86\% of the paraphrases that the router sends to the
correct adapter. Only 36\% are routed, however, so the routed paraphrase accuracy is
32\%. All 636 paraphrases that were not routed passed the entity filter and were
declined by the similarity threshold (median similarity 0.53 against $\tau=0.656$), and
0.2\% were routed to a wrong fact. CounterFact paraphrase prompts begin with an
unrelated sentence, for example ``In 1978 her father suffered a heart attack and died.
The original language of Vishwaroopam was,'' which lowers the similarity to the key.
Since $\tau$ was chosen on unperturbed prompts, a lower threshold for queries with an
exact subject match is a direct remedy. It may reduce abstention on distractors that
contain a stored subject and needs to be tested in a new registered experiment. On
zsRE, the entity filter keeps only the longest matching subject, which is sufficient in
our sample because facts with the same subject have the same answer. A store with
conflicting facts about one subject would require keys that depend on the relation. A
trained router could address both issues, but it would have to preserve
Proposition~\ref{prop:locality}.

\subsection{Canary Prompts}
The KL term and the divergence check use the same two canary prompts. A small $D$ shows
that an adapter leaves those prompts unchanged and does not establish that general
capability is preserved. Unrelated queries are answered by the unmodified base model,
so the remaining risk is limited to the 0.8\% and 3.3\% of distractors that the router
does not decline. A larger held-out set of canary prompts and capability benchmarks on
incorrectly routed queries are needed to evaluate this risk.

\subsection{Cost}
Per-fact adapters require megabytes of storage and several seconds of training per
fact, and our implementation reloads the base model for each candidate. Grouped adapters
reduce storage at the cost of removal granularity, and multi-adapter serving
kernels~\cite{sheng2024slora} would reduce the serving overhead.

\subsection{Scope}
The evaluation uses one base model, one hardware setup, single-token targets, substring
scoring and synthetic distractor names. zsRE is used with reference answers rather than
counterfactual targets. The editing baselines use 200 facts and the other systems 1000.
The saved stores identify the base model by name and not by a hash of its weights. The
controller and vision-language experiments are exploratory.

\subsection{Errors During the Study}
Besides the evaluation error described in Section~\ref{sec:serving}, the record
contains three errors and one instability. An initialization set both LoRA factors to
zero, so no gradient flowed and the vision-language adapters were not trained. A leakage
check for grouped adapters drew held-out facts from inside the training group and
reported a leakage of 100\%. A grouped run at $k=256$ reached 70.7\% instead of 100\%
because it used a different position in the random number stream. Finally, LoRA+ was
unstable for grouped adapters. Each problem was documented before and after it was
corrected.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}

Cerata stores each fact learned after deployment as a separate adapter on a frozen
language model and selects adapters with a router that has no trainable parameters.
With this design, removal restores the previous state exactly and every answer can be
attributed to a stored fact. With 1000 facts each from CounterFact and zsRE, the system
answers more than 99\% of edit prompts with routing, declines most prompts about unknown
subjects, returns to the base model's answers after removal in most cases, and
outperforms instructed retrieval and the chat-served GRACE and WISE implementations on
CounterFact. Paraphrase accuracy on CounterFact is limited by the routing threshold,
conflicting facts about one subject have not been tested, and the divergence check
covers only two prompts. Future work will address the router and the canary set and
will repeat the controller and vision-language experiments at a larger scale.
